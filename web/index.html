How it works

Three steps, about one second.

  1. 1Tell it about the family

    Ages, Social Security, pensions, savings, spending, and what worries them. Ten easy questions.

  2. 2It lives a thousand lifetimes

    Good markets and bad, long lives and short, plus every real year since 1928, with taxes and Medicare done right.

  3. 3It shows what breaks, and the fix

    The one event that would hurt most, in plain words, and the smallest change that makes the plan safe again.

The trick that makes the whole firm possible

A planning engine takes a moment per family. That's fine for one meeting, too slow for 100,000. So a small neural network studied the engine on 75,000 practice households until it could give the same answers thousands of times faster, and it's still checked against the engine whenever it matters.

Questions people ask

Is this financial advice?

No. It's education and decision support: a way to see a plan's weak spots so a family and their advisor can talk about them.

Are these real clients?

No. Every household here is made up. The markets are real: actual stock, bond and inflation history since 1928, and daily S&P 500 closes for the replays.

If I try it on my parents, where does that go?

Nowhere. The planning engine runs inside your browser tab. Nothing you type is sent to a server or saved.

What does "89 out of 100" mean?

It lived out the plan in 1,000 different possible futures. In 89 of every 100, the money lasted as long as they did.

How accurate is the fast brain?

On households it never saw, it lands within about 1.3 points of the full engine. Where it isn't sure, near a cliff edge, the engine checks it again.

Who built this?

Peyton Thalman, a financial advisor who wanted to see why plans break, not just whether they do.
